Product-neutral by design

The framework is six claims, what each depends on, and the gate that closes it. Fivetran and dbt are one way to satisfy those gates, shown because it is the way we can demonstrate end to end. Any tooling that produces the same evidence satisfies the same gate.

Where a client starts

Why the order matters
  • Nothing downstream reads what has not landed, so Move gates everything.
  • Cross-engine parity at Agent depends on the single write at Manage.
  • An agent cannot resolve a definition that Transform has not written and tested.
  • Activate needs something worth writing back, which is why it comes last.
The two catalogs, and the difference

The lake catalog (Polaris, Glue, Unity Catalog, BigLake, OneLake) makes tables reachable: where is this table, and may I read it. dbt Catalog makes them interpretable: what a column means, where it came from, what tests it passed. Both are needed. An estate with only the first has data every engine can find and nobody can explain.

Schema drift, type promotion, auth, pagination, checkpointing, retries and health belong to the platform. Not to a team, and not to a scheduled job somebody inherited.
Why these choices
  • One managed sync, not a pipeline per consumer. Adding a consumer adds no standing compute and no new movement to maintain.
  • The library covers the core estate; the SDK covers the long tail. SAP, Oracle, Salesforce and ServiceNow are in the library. The long tail is the proprietary systems, the in-house application, the shop-floor system, the regional vendor. A large enterprise has hundreds of them, and each one is a connector the SDK can build. They land the same way, on the same managed platform, as everything else.
  • Loading is idempotent. A re-run is always safe; a failed sync retries itself and resumes from its last checkpoint.
  • What lands is a correct, queryable schema. Fivetran's standard shape for application sources, the source's own structure for databases. Whether naming follows Fivetran's convention or the source's is a choice you make per source.
Why it matters commercially
  • One ingestion motion across every source. What used to be a build decision per system is the same move every time.
  • The long tail is where scope gets cut. The core systems are already covered. What shrinks a project is the hundreds of custom sources specific to that business, and that is the part the Connector SDK removes.
  • Nothing here is bespoke, so nothing here is a rewrite next year. Hand-built movement is where the undocumented business rules accumulate.
For your practice
  • The awkward sources stop being the reason a project gets smaller. Your team builds them with the Connector SDK and AI tooling, and they run managed alongside everything else: same syncs, same monitoring, same schema treatment.
  • One predictable ingestion motion. The way a source is landed, monitored and handed over is the same every time, so your team gets faster across every client rather than deep on one.

AI tooling changed the shape of this work, and the honest answer is a range rather than a number. Three modes, and the difference is not the API; it is what the connector has to survive.

!
Where the time actually goes. Not the request loop. It goes into the handful of API behaviours that decide the cursor design, and getting one wrong is a long debugging tail rather than a bug.
  • Inclusive range filters. If both bounds are inclusive, a single-key cursor re-fetches the boundary record on every sync. It needs a compound cursor.
  • Per-sync caps with offset pagination. Advance the offset by what was actually consumed, not by page size, or the off-by-N is structural.
  • Async polling, pagination tokens, query DSLs, streaming, multi-call-per-record. Each changes the shape of the connector rather than adding a step to it.
Profile the API before generating any code. These are interview questions, not discoveries.

Counts and values reconcile against the source, and every schema change that arrived is on the record. Nobody has to take that on trust; it reconciles or it does not, which is why this is the easiest place to start.

✓
Satisfied by evidence, not by tooling. Any pipeline that reconciles counts and values against the source and keeps the schema-change record satisfies this gate.

What the client has when it closes: one governed write per source, with row parity proven against the source and every schema change that arrived on the record.

Do not connect a source nobody has named a consumer for. Landing data because it is easy to land is storage, governance surface and one more thing to explain in an audit, with nothing asking for it. Connect what someone is waiting on, then the next one.

You will also hear: Our regulator says this data cannot leave our network. That is a routing question, answered under Manage.

The catalog in between

Polaris, Glue, Unity Catalog, BigLake, OneLake. It answers two questions: where is this table, and may I read it. That layer is what lets an engine plug in without a new pipeline.

Run the parity query to read the same files through every layer.
Each engine reads the layer it understands, and none of them know about each other. That half of the gate proves the openness claim rather than asserting it.
Why these choices
  • One write, one physical set of files, three ways in. The data is written once as Parquet. Iceberg metadata is always created over it, and Delta Lake metadata as well when you enable it. Two metadata layers over the Parquet, plus the Parquet itself, which anything can read directly.
  • Table maintenance runs on its own schedule, and it is the part nobody wants to own. Snapshots older than the retention period are expired daily, with at least the last two always kept. Delta delete markers are held for up to seven days, or the retention period if that is shorter. Three previous versions of the metadata files are retained. Orphaned files are staged on alternate Saturdays and removed on the following pass. These are documented behaviours of the managed service, not something a client tunes and not something a delivery team writes a job for.
  • What the docs do not claim, this page does not either. There is no user-facing report confirming a maintenance pass ran, which is why maintenance is described here and deliberately absent from the gate.
  • Engine to layer. Databricks reads the Delta log. Snowflake, Athena, BigQuery and DuckDB read the Iceberg metadata. Fabric reads the Parquet directly.
  • Orchestration is dbt's job here. Models run in the order their dependencies require, with run results you can read afterwards, not a cron entry and a hope. That is what makes the arrival of new data and the shaping of it one system rather than two.
Why it matters commercially
  • This is work a client stops paying anyone to build and keep running. Schema evolution, retention and catalog registration are maintenance: necessary, invisible when it works, and the reason data teams never get to the part that was worth funding.
  • It moves the work up-stack. Away from pipeline babysitting, toward the governance and agent layers, which is where the outcome a client is actually paying for gets built.
  • Query-ready on sync completion is the claim clients actually test. It is also what makes the next use case cheap rather than another project.
  • No bespoke scheduler to inherit. Orchestration comes with the platform, so nobody is maintaining a homegrown one and nobody is explaining it to the next team.
For your practice

Fivetran keeps the data current and dbt keeps the jobs in order. Neither is something your team rebuilds at the next client. You arrive with the foundation already running and spend the engagement on the layers above it, which is where the work is worth doing anyway.

The operating model is clean, and a client will ask. Fivetran manages the ingestion and the table layer: reading the source, writing the tables, the merges that apply changes, metadata handling, table maintenance and the managed catalog. The compute to do all of that is Fivetran's, not the client's. In most architectures the merge cost lands on the warehouse bill. The client pays for their own cloud storage, because the data lives in their account, and for whatever compute they point at it to read.

That is the operating model, and it is as far as this page goes. Rates, tiers and actual numbers are a conversation with Fivetran rather than something to quote from an architecture document.

!
"Any cloud, any engine" is architecturally true and has a geography bill. If the lake sits in one region and the engine reads it from another, or from another cloud entirely, that traffic is charged, and it is charged to the client. The architecture permits it; the invoice notices. Establish where the lake lives before anyone attaches an engine somewhere convenient.
!
A catalog the client runs themselves is their cost too. Registering the same tables in Glue, Unity Catalog, BigLake or OneLake is a reasonable thing to do, and often the right thing for survivability. Just book it as a client platform cost rather than assuming it comes with the managed service.

The policy attaches to a source, not to the client. It says that particular data may not be processed through a SaaS service. Most of a client's estate carries no such policy; one or two systems will.

Two models answer it. SaaS processes in Fivetran's cloud. Hybrid Deployment processes inside the client's own network and uses Fivetran's cloud only to orchestrate. Both are secure and governed, and in neither one does Fivetran store or maintain the data. What persists is a cursor marking the last checkpoint, so the next incremental sync knows where to resume.

The question that clarifies it. When a source carries a no-SaaS-processing policy, ask where the data is going to land. It is usually a cloud data platform, which is itself a SaaS service. That is not a gotcha; it is the question that separates a policy about the processing path from a policy about the data leaving the environment at all. Those need different architectures, and clients frequently have not distinguished them.

✕
The managed lake does not support Hybrid Deployment today. Follow the chain and it is absolute: a source whose policy forbids processing through a SaaS service requires Hybrid Deployment, and Hybrid Deployment cannot write to the managed lake. That source cannot land in the lake, and there is no configuration that gets around it.

What it does not do is break the architecture. That source lands in a warehouse destination over Hybrid Deployment; everything else lands in the lake as designed. The client ends up with two destinations, deliberately, which is a normal outcome and not a compromise. Establish which sources carry the policy before the design is drawn and it is a routing decision. Find out afterwards and it is a redesign.

One query, every metadata layer, identical results. That half proves the openness claim rather than asserting it. The second half is what separates orchestration from a schedule: the run results name what ran, in what order, and what it depended on.

Table maintenance (snapshot expiry, delete markers, metadata versions, orphan files) runs automatically and is deliberately not in this gate. There is no documented report, alert or run history confirming a pass completed. Some effects are visible in the client's own storage, and the docs name the orphans folders and say not to touch them, but a folder you can see is not a pass you can verify, and a gate needs the second one.

✓
Satisfied by evidence, not by tooling. Any lake that writes once and serves more than one metadata layer over the same files, with an orchestrator that reports what ran and why, satisfies this gate. The formats are open standards and the gate is the identical result, not who wrote it.

If a client runs exactly one engine and has no second one planned, multi-format is optionality they are not using yet. Say that plainly. It costs nothing to keep and it is not, on its own, a reason to change anything today.

If you say one thing about this stage
One write, one set of Parquet files in your own data lake, with Iceberg metadata over it, Delta metadata when you want it, and the Parquet readable directly.
What this gate lets you sell
One query, every metadata layer, identical results: a demonstration rather than a position. You sell the same proof at every client, whatever engine they already run.
What to verify
Read real rows through each layer and compare values. A row count can be answered from table metadata without touching a data file, so it passes while the reads underneath are broken.

dbt State parses the SQL rather than diffing the file, so a reformat or a renamed alias is not a change. It also asks whether the upstream data moved, not only whether the code did, which is the part a manifest comparison cannot do at all.
Why these choices
  • Staging, marts, then semantic, with source schemas parameterized. One set of SQL runs against more than one warehouse without a fork.
  • The compiled manifest is the artifact, not a diagram someone drew. It carries every model, column, description and dependency, and it is what downstream governance reads.
  • Blast radius before rebuild. You are told exactly what breaks downstream before anything runs. Governance before execution is how teams at scale manage change.
  • How fresh a model has to be becomes a declaration, not a schedule. A per-model freshness tolerance states how stale that table is allowed to get, in the project, where it can be reviewed. Most estates carry the same requirement scattered across cron jobs whose reasons nobody remembers.
  • Materialization is a per-platform trade-off, not a rule. Views are cheaper to maintain on one engine, tables faster on another. The model SQL is identical either way.
  • Search the compiled project, not the files. dbt Wizard answers questions across the compiled manifest (SQL, column descriptions, documentation) in one call, so "where does the word risk appear in this project" has an answer with a source.
  • The engine underneath is changing. Fusion is the new execution engine. Worth knowing it exists, and worth not designing a client's timeline around it yet.
  • The dbt platform runs it. Scheduling, environments and run history come with the platform rather than being assembled per client.
Why it matters commercially
  • This is where the refactor cost lives at a platform change. Logic in the platform refactors entirely. Logic in version control refactors partially.
  • Undocumented business rules accumulate here, which is exactly why hand-built transformation is the most expensive thing to inherit.
  • Governed transformation is the trust foundation for everything above it, agents included. An agent cannot be more reliable than the layer it reads.
  • Mistakes are caught before the warehouse bills for them. Fusion understands SQL across engine dialects, so a broken reference or a bad column surfaces while the model is being written rather than after a run somebody already paid for.
  • You can see which models cost what. Warehouse spend attributed per model turns "the bill went up" into the specific change that caused it, which is the difference between a cost conversation and a cost argument.
  • Staleness a person absorbs, an agent acts on. Someone reading a dashboard notices that yesterday's number looks off. An agent does not pause to ask whether the data is current. It acts, at machine speed, and a tolerance that was survivable for the dashboard is not survivable for the agent.
  • Increasingly this work is done by tooling rather than by people. An agent changing a model reads the same compiled project a senior engineer would (lineage, state, tests) and its work is checked the same way. That is what makes handing the volume to AI defensible rather than reckless.
For your practice

Governed transformation is the layer you build once and run everywhere. The project is the client's; the skills that generate, test and migrate it are yours. One senior practitioner with that tooling delivers what used to take a team and a quarter.

Coverage is countable from the compiled project: either every model carries a test, or you can name the ones that do not. Lineage and blast radius are generated from that same compiled project rather than drawn by hand, so the answer to "what does this affect" is produced rather than remembered.

!
Break one model on purpose and confirm the tests catch it. A check you have never seen fail is an assumption.

A client who is changing engines has a second and stricter gate to pass: value parity, compared as values rather than as a row count. That is a migration question rather than a transformation one, and it is answered under .

✓
Satisfied by evidence, not by tooling. Any transformation layer that keeps logic in version control, generates lineage from the project itself, and has a test suite somebody has watched fail satisfies this gate.

Do not pay for portability a client will never spend. Where a workload genuinely needs an engine's native capability (a function, a procedure, a performance behaviour) use it. Avoiding it costs real expressiveness and buys nothing if that client is never moving. What matters is that the choice is deliberate and documented.

The ungoverned answer is never an error. It returns rows, it looks reasonable, and nobody checks it, which is precisely why the governed definition has to live where the agent reads rather than in somebody's head. The ratio belongs to these weightings; the failure mode does not.
Why these choices
  • Document every column, and test what matters. Documentation coverage is what makes a natural-language answer possible; the tests are what make it trustworthy.
  • dbt Catalog and lineage are what make it findable. Documentation nobody can search is documentation nobody uses, and the same is true for an agent. The catalog turns a compiled project into something a person or an agent can navigate.
  • The Semantic Layer is where a metric is defined once. A governed metric queried through it returns the same answer to a person, a dashboard and an agent, because there is only one definition to return.
  • Resolve before acting. The agent reads the governed definition, then writes the query.
  • The loop closes in the project. Change the definition in dbt, re-ingest, and the next answer uses it. No prompt edit, and the lineage moves with it.
Why it matters commercially
  • A wrong answer delivered fluently costs more than no answer. It is acted on before anyone checks it.
  • This is the layer clients cannot buy off the shelf. The definitions are the client's; the tooling that keeps them correct is what you bring.
  • Agreeing definitions once makes the second use case cheap. Every agent after the first inherits them instead of relitigating what a number means.
  • Explainability survives procurement. Lineage, tests and documentation are what a client shows when someone asks why they trusted the number, and they are the same artifacts that make the AI work defensible.
For your practice

The definitions belong to each client. The skill that resolves, tests and republishes them is yours, and it runs the same everywhere, so you sell a working agent rather than the weeks it used to take to agree what a customer is.

An Agents Schema built out of dbt's compiled artifacts resolves definitions directly. That is the emerging shape and it is on the roadmap, not something to build a client on today. The supported path is the dbt MCP server, and which tool family you reach for depends on the question being asked.

A definition that changes without a re-ingest. The governed layer is a copy of what the project compiled. Why these choices
  • Three agent backends answered the same question and the top answers matched. Not because they agree, but because all three read the same underlying files.
  • Ten engines, five catalogs, three open formats, one write, zero copies. Raw SQL, no agent and no semantic layer in the path, and every number identical.
  • A laptop counts. An in-process engine reads the same tables through the same catalog as the warehouse did. No cluster, no warehouse, no standing cost.
  • Adding an engine is a configuration change measured in minutes. The slowest we timed was 35, and that included first-run account setup.

Four of the ten were attached to a live lake and timed. All four used the same catalog and the same vended credentials, and none needed custom code. They differed only in connection syntax.

07 · What ownership actually buys

The data is your client's, and that pays continuously

In a proprietary store, data is readable only by the compute that owns it. Every question has to be asked through one vendor's engine, on that vendor's meter, at whatever the contract says next year. Open formats in the client's own storage end that.

What that is worth on an ordinary Tuesday

The payoff most people miss is not the big migration; it is the small choices, every week. An analytics engineer wants to check whether a model change does what they think. Today that means running it on the warehouse and burning credits to find out. With the data in open formats, they point an in-process engine at the same tables on a laptop and find out for free. Same data, same governed tables, no cluster, no consumption.

The same logic scales up. A workload that suits a different engine gets that engine. A team that needs an ad-hoc query surface gets one without a licence conversation. None of that is a migration; it is what having options looks like day to day.

And when a bigger change does come up

A contract comes up for renewal. An acquisition brings a second stack through the door. A team needs an engine the current platform cannot give them. The question is always the same: what would it actually cost to change? Most architectures cannot answer it. This one can, and the answer has two halves.

What is gone
The data does not move. The raw layer already sits in open formats in the client's own storage, so this is not a data platform migration. Nothing is exported, re-loaded or reconciled.
What is left
Re-pointing the transformation project and its SQL at a different engine. How much that costs depends on how platform-specific the project was allowed to become.

Five migrations, 57 decisions, 19 models. Sixteen rewritten with no human edits, six first-attempt failures, and values identical on eighteen of nineteen, measured 30 July 2026.
What was measured

Five dbt projects across pharma and retail, migrated Snowflake to Databricks and Databricks to Snowflake: both directions at every band, nineteen models in total. One Fivetran-managed lake held the raw layer throughout and was never rewritten; only the transformation project moved. Fusion drove every run, with the Databricks side on a SQL warehouse rather than an all-purpose cluster. Source tables of 750 rows and one derived fact of 6,000: enough to prove pattern coverage and merge behaviour, and nothing at all about tuning at production scale.

Where the measurement disagreed with the read
!
The tests are not what tells you a rewrite is correct. A model was broken on purpose (one aggregate rounded to a different place) and the run succeeded, its whole test suite passed, and the row count matched. Only a value-level comparison against the engine being left caught it. A green run, a green test suite and a matching row count are three layers of agreement that can all be wrong at once.

Two corrections. Reading a project tells you what to decide; it does not tell you what the target engine will refuse. Several of the top band's patterns only appeared once the migrated code ran, so budget that band for a run and not only a read. And verification does not always end in agreement: one model of nineteen cannot match, for reasons in the arithmetic rather than in the code. A quantified, documented difference is a result, not a failure, and it is worth knowing before promising bit-exact parity.

!
Incremental behaviour can change with the runtime, not just the engine. The same project can run incrementally on one runtime and silently fall back to full table materialisation on another. If the modelled layers are incremental-heavy, that is the first thing to establish. It moves a client between bands on its own, and it is invisible until the bill arrives.
What always changes
  • Re-point the project at the new adapter and target.
  • Give the engine access to the lake tables and catalog.
  • Update source, database and schema naming for the target catalog.
  • Refactor platform-specific SQL and macros. This is the band-defining work.
  • Revisit incremental models and materialisations.
  • Re-test row counts, tests, performance and downstream reporting before anyone trusts a number again.
i
The band no longer sets the timeline the way it used to. A dialect rewrite is high-volume, low-judgement work, the kind AI tooling does well, and dbt is what makes letting it run safe: the compiled graph and a value-level comparison against the engine you are leaving say whether each rewritten model is right. You can automate the rewrite because you can verify it, and that is the general rule rather than a fact about migrations. How much of any delivery can run unattended is set by how much of it you can gate.
For your practice
Two engagements live here, and the small one comes first. Helping a client actually use their optionality (the right engine per workload, a free one for development) is delivery work you can start immediately. The larger one arrives at renewal: vendor management, procurement and supply chain are measured on leverage, and they are almost never consulted on the architecture decision that creates it. Walk in with a priced answer to "what would it cost to move" and the client has a negotiating position they did not have before, on a stack you also deliver. "Storage separated from compute" is already their language. The point is not to move the client; it is that a client with options negotiates differently from one without them.
When this is the wrong call
Portability is insurance, and insurance is not the argument. A client running one engine, with no second platform in view and no renewal in front of them, gets far more from what this changes in an ordinary week than from a move they may never make. Lead with the Tuesday, not the migration.

Five stages, and the next one to sell

A map to read, not a form to fill in. The framework carries no assessment and scores nothing. You do not need every client at stage 4; you need to know which stage they are in and sell the next one.

i
The band shifts between stage 2 and stage 3, from trusted data to trusted AI and agents. That is the jump most organizations are standing in front of right now, and it is the one the open, portable foundation exists to make.
Reframe, do not rebuild

Teams already delivering Fivetran, dbt and a warehouse are already delivering most of this. What changes is the narrative and the order, not the skill set.

Point the gates at finished work

Every client you have built for is running something that is drifting right now: sources change, schemas evolve, platforms release, and the person who understood the original design has moved on. Re-running a gate against a finished engagement finds that drift, and the finding is the conversation. No new entrant can do this; it needs the delivery history.

Three ways this goes wrong

Positioning the open lake as replacing the client's platform (it damages the relationship with the platform vendor, so present both paths). Pushing open formats at a client who is not ready (qualify first). And treating it as a two-party conversation when the platform vendor should be at the table: the message is about expanding the data footprint, not competing for it.
